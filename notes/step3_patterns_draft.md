## Draft rules of thumb

1. **Leverage only held where the target needed more than 5 years to replace supply.** K1 is the only case that partly worked, and the only one scored high on time to replace. In every case where the target could replace supply within about 5 years (K2–K4), the leverage failed or backfired.
2. **Price or supply management fails outright when substitutes are easy.** Both cases with high substitutability (K2 copper, K3 tin) never gained, or quickly lost, any hold over the market. K3 also shows that propping up prices draws in new supply and substitutes, which leaves the group worse off.
3. **Near-monopoly plus state control is not enough if the target can diversify within a few years.** K4 had the strongest-looking profile (high concentration, low substitutability, high state control), but a sharp coercive move used for a narrow political aim taught Japan to diversify. The leverage was spent in one use.
4. **Even a cut that works makes buyers organise.** K1 partly worked, but it led directly to the IEA, stockpiles and new non-Arab supply, which wore the leverage down over the following decade. Expect any visible threat to speed up the target's replacement.

## Variables that don't separate successes from failures

- **Dependence on the target:** high in K1 (partly worked) and in K2 and K4 (failed or backfired). It doesn't separate them.
- **State control:** high in both K1 (partly worked) and K4 (backfired). It doesn't separate them.
- **Cohesion (counted):** low in both K1 (partly worked) and K4 (backfired), and medium in the two failures. If anything it runs the "wrong" way, so the actor count may not capture what holds a group together.
- **Concentration:** medium in three of the four cases. The highest score was in K4, which backfired. On its own it doesn't predict success.
- **What does separate them (so far):** **time to replace**, and, for the pure failures, **substitutability**.
- **Caveat:** with only 4–5 cases these are suggestions, not tests. One changed score can flip a pattern.

## Factors the six variables may be missing

- **The kind of demand.** A specific political ask with a face-saving path (K1: disengagement talks) differs from a demand the target can't meet without losing face (K4, K5), and from a price target (K2, K3).
- **Whether the supplier gains even without a concession.** Oil producers earned far more from higher prices whatever the US did. The tin council and CIPEC had no such cushion.
- **Market conditions at the time.** The 1973 oil market was tight. The tin and copper markets of the mid-1970s and 1980s were slack.
- **Stockpiles and money.** US stockpiles (tin) undercut the supplier, and the tin council collapsed when its borrowed money ran out. Neither is one of the six variables.
- **Leakage through intermediaries.** Oil companies redirected cargoes, which spread the embargo's impact across all buyers.
- **Uncertainty over intent.** In K4 it is disputed whether China deliberately targeted Japan. Ambiguous coercion may have different effects from openly declared coercion.

## Caveats to state in the write-up

- **Who did the scoring.** Claude drafted the K1–K4 scores and Andrew reviewed them. Ayodele scored K5. Each case has a single final scorer, so there is no inter-rater agreement figure.
- **Possible bias.** The scorer knew the outcomes while scoring, so the scores may be biased towards fitting them.
- **How well the lessons transfer.** Four of the five cases are commodities, and chips are harder to substitute.
