## K1 · Arab oil embargo, 1973–74 (target: the US, plus the Netherlands)

**Summary**
- **What the supplier wanted:** in October 1973, during the Arab–Israeli war, Arab OPEC members cut production and embargoed the US and the Netherlands. The aim was to make Israel's backers press for withdrawal from the territories occupied in 1967.
- **How the target responded:** the US kept supplying Israel. It then brokered the Egypt–Israel (January 1974) and Syria–Israel (May 1974) disengagements, and led consumer countries in founding the International Energy Agency (November 1974), which included an emergency oil-sharing system.
- **How long it lasted:** the embargo on the US was lifted on 18 March 1974, after about 5 months, and on the Netherlands in July 1974. The price rise it helped trigger, roughly a fourfold increase, lasted much longer.

**Outcome: partly worked.** It pulled the US into active Middle East diplomacy and produced a large, lasting price and revenue gain. It did not get withdrawal to the 1967 lines, and it prompted buyers to organise (the IEA) and diversify.

| Variable | Score | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Concentration | medium | Arab producers supplied roughly half of world oil exports, but a smaller share of US imports. This is borderline with high if world exports are used. |
| Substitutability | medium | Non-Arab producers (Iran, Venezuela, Nigeria) and oil companies redirecting cargoes covered part of the gap, but spare capacity was thin. |
| Time to replace | high | Building new US and allied supply (Alaska, North Sea) took until the late 1970s, and US import dependence kept rising. |
| Cohesion | low | 4 members (Saudi Arabia, Kuwait, Libya, Iraq) were needed to reach about 80% of Arab output. The figures are approximate, and Iraq did not join the production cuts. |
| Dependence on the target | high | Saudi Arabia relied on the US for security, even though the US took a small share of Arab oil exports. This follows the "take the higher of markets or security" rule, but it is group-level and mixed. |
| State control | high | Producer governments set production levels and ordered the cuts, even where foreign firms operated the fields. |

**Sources**
- US Office of the Historian, "Oil Embargo, 1973–1974": https://history.state.gov/milestones/1969-1976/oil-embargo
- Britannica, "Arab oil embargo": https://www.britannica.com/event/Arab-oil-embargo
- IEA, history of the agency (founded November 1974): https://www.iea.org/about/history

## K2 · CIPEC copper exporters, from 1967 (target: copper-importing countries)

**Summary**
- **What the supplier wanted:** Chile, Peru, Zambia and Zaire (DR Congo) formed CIPEC in Lusaka in June 1967, inspired by OPEC. They wanted more control over copper trade and higher, steadier export earnings.
- **How the target responded:** importers barely needed to respond. Producers outside CIPEC (notably the US, Canada and Australia), recycled scrap copper, and substitution (for example, aluminium) kept supply flowing.
- **How long it lasted:** CIPEC never gained real price power. Its one coordinated move, a 10% export cut agreed in November 1974 and later raised to 15%, did not lift prices in the 1975–76 slump. The council faded and was dissolved around 1990 (sources differ on the year).

**Outcome: failed.** It never worked, but it didn't backfire either, so "failed" was added as a fourth outcome category alongside worked, partly worked and backfired.

| Variable | Score | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Concentration | medium | The founding members held a large share of world copper **exports**, but far less of world output, because the US and Canada were big producers outside the group. |
| Substitutability | high | Copper was a commodity with many producers outside CIPEC, plus scrap and aluminium substitution. |
| Time to replace | medium | Importers could lean on the US, Canada and others, and new mines take several years. With substitutability high, this matters less. |
| Cohesion | medium | 3 members (Chile, Zambia, Zaire) made up about 80% of the group's output. The figures are approximate. |
| Dependence on the target | high | Copper was most of export earnings for Zambia and Chile, and the industrialised importers were the main markets. |
| State control | medium | Nationalisation was under way when CIPEC was founded (Zaire 1967, Chile's "Chileanisation" from 1967, Zambia 1969), but was not complete in 1967. |

**Sources**
- K. Mingst, "Cooperation or illusion: an examination of the Intergovernmental Council of Copper Exporting Countries", *International Organization* 30(2), 1976: https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/journals/international-organization/article/cooperation-or-illusion-an-examination-of-the-intergovernmental-council-of-copper-exporting-countries/C561E07B48B716F3D7CFC9E615363928
- CIA, "The CIPEC Countries and the Free World Copper Market" (declassified): https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/node/1953704
- Economic History Society, "Zambia, CIPEC and the Global South": https://ehs.org.uk/zambia-cipec-and-the-global-south-visions-for-development-in-the-long-1960s/

## K3 · International Tin Council, 1985 (target: tin buyers / the world market)

**Summary**
- **What the supplier wanted:** under the International Tin Agreements, producer and consumer governments used a buffer stock (buying tin when prices fell) plus export quotas to hold the tin price above a floor. In the early 1980s that floor was set too high for a weakening market.
- **How the target responded:** buyers used less tin (thinner tinplate, aluminium cans). The US, which was outside the agreement, sold from its strategic stockpile, and non-members such as Brazil expanded output.
- **How long it lasted:** the support held prices up for about four years on borrowed money. On 24 October 1985 the buffer stock ran out of funds and defaulted on about £900 million, the London Metal Exchange suspended tin trading, and the price collapsed. The council was later wound up.

**Outcome: backfired.** Prices ended far lower than before, the council collapsed into years of lawsuits, and producers lost the market share they had given up to non-members.

| Variable | Score | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Concentration | medium | The producer members (Malaysia, Indonesia, Thailand and others) held most but not all non-communist output. Bolivia and Brazil were outside. |
| Substitutability | high | Aluminium and plastics replaced tinplate in many uses, and non-member producers were expanding. |
| Time to replace | medium | Buyers never had to build their own supply, because the market was in surplus. The US strategic stockpile was existing inventory, not new supply, and opening new mines takes several years. |
| Cohesion | medium | 3 producers (Malaysia, Indonesia, Thailand) made up about 80% of members' output. The figures are approximate. |
| Dependence on the target | medium | Tin was an important but not dominant export for the main members, for example Malaysia, whose exports had diversified. |
| State control | medium | The council could set export quotas, and some producers were state-owned (for example, Indonesia's PT Timah), but much mining was private. |

**Sources**
- M. Prest, "The Collapse of the International Tin Agreement", *IDS Bulletin* 17(4), 1986: https://www.ids.ac.uk/publications/the-collapse-of-the-international-tin-agreement
- I. A. Mallory, "Conduct Unbecoming: The Collapse of the International Tin Agreement", *American University International Law Review* (1990)
- The Northern Miner, "The tin metal scheme that went wrong": https://www.northernminer.com/news/the-tin-metal-scheme-that-went-wrong/1000123981/

## K4 · China's rare-earth squeeze on Japan, 2010 (target: Japan)

**Summary**
- **What the supplier wanted:** in September 2010, Japan detained a Chinese trawler captain after a collision near the Senkaku/Diaoyu islands. Traders reported that Chinese rare-earth shipments to Japan stopped. The apparent aim was the captain's release, and backing for China's position on the islands. China denied any embargo.
- **How the target responded:** Japan released the captain within weeks. It then diversified: it funded non-Chinese supply (Lynas in Australia), stockpiled, recycled and cut rare-earth use. It also joined the US and EU in a WTO case that China lost in 2014.
- **How long it lasted:** shipments were back to normal by late November 2010, about two months later. Japan's dependence on China fell over the following years from about 90%.

**Outcome: backfired.** The captain was released quickly, but the main lasting effect was that Japan diversified and China lost the WTO case. Note: whether China deliberately targeted Japan is disputed, because China had already cut its worldwide export quotas in mid-2010 (King and Armstrong, 2013).

| Variable | Score | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Concentration | high | China produced the vast majority of the world's rare earths, and supplied about 90% of Japan's imports. |
| Substitutability | low | No other producer operated at scale in 2010. Mountain Pass in the US was closed, and Lynas was not yet producing. |
| Time to replace | medium | Lynas began shipping in 2012–13, and Japan cut its dependence substantially within a few years. |
| Cohesion | low | Chinese output was spread across many firms, including small and illegal miners. Under the count rule this scores low, even though the state controlled exports (see state control). |
| Dependence on the target | high | Japan took well over 30% of China's rare-earth exports, probably about half or more. It imported about 23,300 t from China in 2010, against roughly 35,000 t of Chinese exports in January–November 2010. This is a rough estimate from two sources that may count products differently. Rare earths were a tiny part of China's economy, so this market-share measure may overstate China's real dependence on Japan. |
| State control | high | The government set export quotas and licences and controlled customs clearance. |

**Sources**
- A. King and S. Armstrong, "Did China really ban rare earth metals exports to Japan?", East Asia Forum, 18 Aug 2013: https://eastasiaforum.org/2013/08/18/did-china-really-ban-rare-earth-metals-exports-to-japan/
- NATO StratCom COE, "Hybrid threats: 2010 Senkaku crisis" (timeline, including exports fully restored on 29 Nov): https://stratcomcoe.org/cuploads/pfiles/senkaku_crisis.pdf
- Mining.com, "China-Japan rare earth spat curbs exports" (2010): https://www.mining.com/china-japan-rare-earth-spat-curbs-exports/
- VOA, \"China rare earths exports up in 2010\" (China Commerce Ministry figures, about 35,000 t in January–November 2010): https://voa.pangea-cms.com/preview/en-US/a/china-rare-earths-exports-up-in-2010-114128034/168512.html
- LAEX, \"Tokyo's strategy to counter China's rare earths grip\" (Japan imported about 23,300 t from China in 2010, 82% of its rare-earth imports): https://laex.in/daily-issue/tokyos-strategy-to-counter-chinas-rare-earths-grip/

## K5 · Japan's materials restrictions on Korea, 2019–2023 (target: South Korea)

**Summary**
- **What the supplier wanted:** in July 2019 Japan ended bulk export licences to Korea for three chipmaking chemicals: hydrogen fluoride, photoresists and fluorinated polyimide. In August it removed Korea from its trusted-trade "white list". Officially the reason was export-control concerns. It was widely read as pressure over the 2018 Korean court rulings that ordered Japanese firms to compensate wartime forced labourers.
- **How the target responded:** Korea did not reverse the rulings. It filed a WTO complaint (September 2019), struck Japan off its own white list, and funded domestic production. Korean chipmakers switched much of their hydrogen fluoride to local and other suppliers.
- **How long it lasted:** the controls stayed for almost four years. They were lifted in March 2023, after Korea's new government offered a plan in which a Korean foundation pays the forced-labour victims. Korea then withdrew its WTO case, and both sides restored each other's white-list status in mid-2023.

**Outcome: backfired.**
- **Why backfired:** Japanese hydrogen fluoride exports to Korea fell sharply and Korean domestic production rose (RIETI study), so Japanese suppliers lost a market. Korea did not concede while the controls were in force.
- **Alternative reading (partly worked):** the 2023 Korean compensation plan is roughly what Japan wanted, although it came with a change of government and US pressure for Japan–Korea security cooperation.

| Variable | Score | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Concentration | high | Japan supplied about 90% of the fluorinated polyimide and photoresist used by Korean chipmakers, and about 70% of their hydrogen fluoride. |
| Substitutability | medium | Hydrogen fluoride could be sourced elsewhere or made locally after quality testing. Leading-edge photoresists had few non-Japanese makers. The RIETI study found substitution happened only for hydrogen fluoride. |
| Time to replace | medium | Korean hydrogen fluoride production scaled up within about 1–2 years, but resists and polyimide took longer. Boundary of low and medium for hydrogen fluoride. |
| Cohesion | low | Across the three chemicals, 4 or more Japanese firms were involved (for example Stella Chemifa and Morita for hydrogen fluoride; JSR, Tokyo Ohka and Shin-Etsu for resists). Per chemical it would be 2–3. |
| Dependence on the target | high | Korea was a major customer for these Japanese chemicals, because its chipmakers are among the world's biggest buyers. There was no security dependence on Korea. |
| State control | medium | METI's licensing powers were used, and in practice slowed exports, but the producers are private firms. One could argue high, because the government showed it could restrict exports at will. |

**Sources**
- Baker McKenzie Sanctions News, METI's 2019 licensing changes for exports to Korea: https://sanctionsnews.bakermckenzie.com/additional-restrictions-added-to-metis-licensing-policies-and-procedures-on-exports-of-controlled-items-to-the-republic-of-korea
- Makioka and Zhang (RIETI, 2023), effects of the export controls on Japan–Korea trade and Korean production: https://www.rieti.go.jp/en/publications/summary/23030011.html
- Korea Times, "Korea withdraws WTO complaint about Japan's export curb", 16 Mar 2023: https://www.koreatimes.co.kr/foreignaffairs/20230316/korea-withdraws-wto-complaint-about-japans-export-curb

# Cases added in November 2026

The five cases above are all cuts or price schemes, so none tested whether attaching **conditions** to supply works, which is what the paper recommends, and only one of them worked. Four cases were added to fill that gap. Each was chosen because the leverage took the form of a condition or bargain, or because it involved allies and the US over export controls.

Who the target is, and what the target needs, was fixed **before** scoring (this commit), together with the wider wording of "time to replace" and the "form of leverage" label in `notes/variable_anchors.md`.

| Case | Group using the leverage | Target | What the target needs | Form of leverage |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| K6 · Nuclear supply safeguards, from 1974 | Nuclear suppliers in the Nuclear Suppliers Group, with Canada and Australia leading on safeguards | Importing states, for example Japan and members of the European Community | Uranium, fuel and nuclear technology | Condition |
| K7 · EU data flows and US surveillance, 2020–2023 | The EU | The US government | Lawful transfers of EU personal data to US firms | Condition |
| K8 · CoCom, 1949–1994 | Western allies, including the US | The Soviet bloc | Advanced Western technology | Cut |
| K9 · Dutch and Japanese chip controls, 2023 | The Netherlands and Japan | The US | Allied chip tools kept out of China | Condition (a bargain over alignment) |

K9's ending is not known yet. It is scored on the six variables, but its outcome is recorded as "too early", and it is left out of the rules of thumb and the closest-case comparison.

Labels for the original five: K1 cut or threat to cut; K2 supply or price management; K3 supply or price management; K4 cut or threat to cut; K5 cut or threat to cut.

## K6 · Nuclear supply safeguards, from 1974 (target: importing states such as Japan and the European Community)

**Summary**
- **What the supplier wanted:** after India used Canadian-supplied technology for its 1974 nuclear test, suppliers made nuclear exports conditional on stronger safeguards against military use. Canada demanded new safeguards agreements from its buyers, the Nuclear Suppliers Group first met in 1975 and published guidelines in 1978, and in May 1977 Australia announced that its uranium would go only to countries meeting its safeguards conditions.
- **How the target responded:** Canada halted uranium shipments to the European Community and Japan around the turn of 1977 while new agreements were negotiated. Both accepted Canada's terms: shipments to the Community resumed in July 1977 under an interim agreement, and Canada and Japan signed new safeguards arrangements in December 1977.
- **How long it lasted:** safeguards conditions have been a standard part of nuclear supply ever since. The exception is India, which refused, was cut off, built its own programme, and in 2008 won a waiver from the suppliers under US pressure.

**Outcome: worked.** The buyers accepted the conditions within months, and the condition outlived the episode by decades.

| Variable | Score | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Concentration | high | The suppliers that tightened together (Canada, the US, France and others) provided most of the uranium and almost all of the enrichment Japan and the Community relied on. The shares are approximate. |
| Substitutability | low | The main suppliers all moved to tougher safeguards at the same time, and suppliers outside the group were small. |
| Time to replace | high | Japan has no uranium of its own, and building a full independent fuel supply takes well over five years. |
| Cohesion | medium | About three suppliers (the US, Canada and France) made up most of what these buyers needed. The figures are approximate. |
| Dependence on the target | high | Japan and the Community were among Canada's main uranium customers. There was no security dependence on them. |
| State control | high | Nuclear exports need government licences, and Canada's uranium industry included a state-owned firm, Eldorado Nuclear. |

**Sources**
- Arms Control Association, "The Nuclear Suppliers Group at a Glance": https://www.armscontrol.org/factsheets/nuclear-suppliers-group-nsg-glance
- Australian Senate committee report, "Safeguards policy: development": https://www.aph.gov.au/Parliamentary_Business/Committees/Senate/Former_Committees/uranium/report/c06-1
- Japan–Canada–IAEA protocol, 2 December 1977 (The World and Japan database): https://worldjpn.net/documents/texts/JPSC/19771202.T1E.html
- The Press (Christchurch), "Canada, Common Market near nuclear accord", 18 February 1977: https://paperspast.natlib.govt.nz/newspapers/CHP19770218.2.66

## K7 · EU data flows and US surveillance, 2020–2023 (target: the US government)

**Summary**
- **What the supplier wanted:** in July 2020 the EU's top court (the Schrems II ruling) struck down the Privacy Shield, the legal basis for moving EU personal data to the US, because US surveillance law gave Europeans too little protection and no real redress. The EU wanted those safeguards before data could flow freely again.
- **How the target responded:** the US government changed its own intelligence rules. Executive Order 14086 (7 October 2022) limited signals intelligence to listed legitimate purposes and created a Data Protection Review Court for complaints from EU residents.
- **How long it lasted:** the EU then recognised the new US safeguards as adequate on 10 July 2023 (the EU–US Data Privacy Framework). The EU General Court upheld that decision on 3 September 2025, although an appeal is possible.

**Outcome: worked.** The US changed rules on a sensitive security matter in order to keep data flowing. Critics argue the changes don't go far enough, and the framework's long-term survival is still being tested in court.

| Variable | Score | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Concentration | high | Only the EU can permit transfers of EU personal data, and it is the largest partner in US cross-border data flows. |
| Substitutability | medium | Other legal routes, such as standard contract clauses with extra safeguards, survived the ruling but became uncertain and costly. |
| Time to replace | medium | The route around the EU was for US firms to keep EU data inside Europe. Large firms began doing so, but rebuilding services that way takes years. |
| Cohesion | high | One actor: a binding ruling by the EU's court, applied across all member states. |
| Dependence on the target | high | The EU relies on the US for security through NATO. |
| State control | high | The ruling bound firms directly, and regulators enforced it: Ireland's data protection authority fined Meta €1.2 billion in 2023 over its transfers. |

**Sources**
- Executive Order 14086, "Enhancing Safeguards for United States Signals Intelligence Activities", 7 October 2022 (American Presidency Project): https://www.presidency.ucsb.edu/documents/executive-order-14086-enhancing-safeguards-for-united-states-signals-intelligence
- Wilson Sonsini, "EU court upholds the validity of the EU–U.S. Data Privacy Framework" (2025): https://www.wsgr.com/en/insights/eu-court-upholds-the-validity-of-the-eu-us-data-privacy-framework.html
- Privacy Company, "Latombe v. European Commission": https://www.privacycompany.eu/blog/latombe-v-european-commission-the-dpf-ca-tombe

## K8 · CoCom, 1949–1994 (target: the Soviet bloc)

**Summary**
- **What the supplier wanted:** Western allies, including the US, agreed lists of technology they would not export to the Soviet bloc, to slow its military development.
- **How the target responded:** the Soviet bloc built its own industries where it could and tried to obtain controlled technology through neutral countries, front companies and espionage. In the Toshiba–Kongsberg affair, the Japanese and Norwegian firms sold the USSR propeller-milling machines in 1982–84, disguised as less advanced equipment. The machines helped make Soviet submarines quieter. The US banned imports from Kongsberg and Congress moved to sanction Toshiba.
- **How long it lasted:** the regime held for 45 years and ended in March 1994, after the Cold War. Its lists passed to the Wassenaar Arrangement.

**Outcome: partly worked.** It slowed Soviet access to advanced technology for decades, but leaks like Toshiba–Kongsberg showed its limits.

| Variable | Score | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Concentration | high | The members held most of the world's advanced computing, electronics and machine-tool supply. |
| Substitutability | medium | Neutral countries outside the regime, smuggling and espionage covered part of the gap. |
| Time to replace | high | The Soviet bloc never fully caught up in computing and microelectronics. |
| Cohesion | low | Many separate members (the US, Japan, West Germany, the UK, France and others) had to hold the line. |
| Dependence on the target | low | The members traded little with the Soviet bloc and faced it as a security threat, not a partner. |
| State control | medium | Each government licensed exports, but enforcement varied. Japan's trade ministry approved the Toshiba sales without question. |

**Sources**
- Christian Science Monitor, on the Toshiba–Kongsberg affair, 11 September 1987: https://csmonitor.com/1987/0911/fshiba-f.html
- GlobalSecurity, "Toshiba–Kongsberg incident": https://www.globalsecurity.org/intell/world/russia/toshiba.htm
- Wikipedia, "Coordinating Committee for Multilateral Export Controls" (dates of the regime): https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CoCom
- Mastanduno, M. (1992). *Economic Containment: CoCom and the Politics of East-West Trade*. Cornell University Press.

## K9 · Dutch and Japanese chip controls, 2023 (target: the US)

**Summary**
- **What the supplier wanted:** in January 2023, after months of US pressure, the Netherlands and Japan agreed to restrict exports of advanced chipmaking equipment to China. In return they shaped the terms: each wrote its own rules (the Dutch announced theirs in March 2023, Japan's took effect in July 2023), neither named China, and the deal's exact contents were never published.
- **How the target responded:** in December 2024 the US extended its rules to cover chipmaking equipment made abroad with US technology, but exempted Japan and the Netherlands, which sources tie to their having already aligned.
- **How long it lasted:** the arrangement is still running.

**Outcome: too early.** The case is scored on the six variables, but left out of the rules of thumb and the closest-case comparison.

| Variable | Score | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Concentration | high | ASML and Japanese toolmakers hold most of the equipment the US wanted kept from China (see the Step 1 screen). |
| Substitutability | low | There are no other suppliers of the most advanced lithography tools. |
| Time to replace | low | The US has a legal route around the allies: rules covering foreign-made equipment that contains US technology. It used that route for other countries in December 2024. |
| Cohesion | medium | Two actors, the Netherlands and Japan. |
| Dependence on the target | high | Both rely on the US for their security. |
| State control | medium | Both governments license these exports, but the firms are private. |

**Sources**
- CSIS, "Japan and the Netherlands Announce Plans for New Export Controls on Semiconductor Equipment" (2023): https://www.csis.org/analysis/japan-and-netherlands-announce-plans-new-export-controls-semiconductor-equipment
- CSIS, "Clues to the U.S.-Dutch-Japanese Semiconductor Export Controls Deal Are Hiding in Plain Sight": https://www.csis.org/analysis/clues-us-dutch-japanese-semiconductor-export-controls-deal-are-hiding-plain-sight
- CSIS, "Understanding the Biden Administration's Updated Export Controls" (December 2024): https://www.csis.org/analysis/understanding-biden-administrations-updated-export-controls
